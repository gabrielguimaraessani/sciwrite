## Purpose, scope, and process: P01–P14

### P01 — Communicate clearly

Make the message understandable without decoding the prose. Preserve scientific
complexity while removing avoidable linguistic difficulty. Engage through relevance
and concrete explanation, not sensational claims. Check whether a reader can state
the passage's point after one reading.

### P02 — Preserve scientific meaning

Check what was done, found, and demonstrated before and after editing. Preserve
actors, populations, comparisons, magnitudes, conditions, limitations, and uncertainty.
Never convert association to causation or inference to fact. Flag apparent technical
errors rather than fixing them without supporting evidence.

### P03 — Fit the audience

Match explanation to readers' likely knowledge, including adjacent-field specialists.
Define unfamiliar concepts briefly and introduce detail gradually. Do not assume
every reader knows specialist acronyms. When unspecified, use an educated scientific
audience as a stated, revisable assumption.

### P04 — Attribute words and ideas

Use citations and quotation marks where appropriate; paraphrase from understanding,
not synonym replacement. Inspect provided comparison sources. Textual overlap needs
context, not an automatic plagiarism verdict. Style changes prove neither plagiarism
nor originality.

### P05 — Exercise contextual judgment

Use active voice, short sentences, and simple words as preferences. Respect genre,
language, verified journal rules, disciplinary conventions, and authorial choices.
Explain the reader-facing benefit of an edit. Leave effective departures from a
stylistic rule alone.

### P06 — Identify question and message

State what the work asks and what readers should understand at the end. Prioritize
arguments and findings accordingly. Label inferred purposes as inference; never
invent a missing study aim. Assess alignment only across material actually available.

### P07 — Build a logical outline

Map main ideas and relationships before polishing. Use general-to-specific,
problem-to-answer, temporal, or causal order where supported. Give concrete moves
rather than “improve flow.” Do not force all genres into one outline.

### P08 — Make writing manageable

When planning is requested, break work into realistic steps and optional daily
targets. Organizing displays and drafting Results and Methods before finishing
Introduction, Discussion, and Abstract is useful, not compulsory. Do not create
reminders or user commitments from a writing-plan request.

### P09 — Orient before detailing

Provide overview and main point before specialized detail when useful. Use concrete
examples to ground abstractions. Respect section functions: a conventional research
introduction need not reveal results simply to give the punch line early.

### P10 — Locate relevant information

Group related content; move distracting technical detail to a useful destination.
Distinguish repetition from information required for interpretation or replication.
Identify exact passages and moves. Never suppress unfavorable evidence to streamline
the story.

### P11 — Give each paragraph one purpose

State its idea early and develop support, explanation, or qualification. Start a new
paragraph when focus changes. Two to five sentences can be a reference, not a limit.
Name the paragraph's purpose to detect content that belongs elsewhere.

### P12 — Let logic carry flow

Make each sentence and paragraph prepare the next. Use connectors for real relations,
not missing premises. Check temporal, general-to-specific, or premise-to-conclusion
progression. Do not add unsupported causal or inferential links.

### P13 — Make openings and endings functional

Open to orient; close to consolidate or prepare the next idea. Remove empty
announcements and mechanical recap. Prefer information to decoration; every technical
paragraph does not need a rhetorical flourish.

### P14 — Revise structure before wording

Check argument, support, and order before polishing sentences. Summarize each
sentence/paragraph's upshot to locate repetition and sequencing problems. Prioritize
structural changes; deduplicate local issues that disappear after a move or cut.

## Words and sentences: P15–P31

### P15 — Cut clutter, not meaning

Remove words with no role in meaning, precision, emphasis, or flow. Replace wordy
phrases and empty openings. Test every deletion in context; minimum word count is
not an independent objective. Keep “estimated” when the value is an estimate.

### P16 — Remove redundancy; keep keywords stable

Delete repeated ideas without informational gain. Keep stable names for diseases,
groups, variables, and concepts. Never force a synonym for variety if it blurs
identity. Useful repetition is especially important across independently read units.

### P17 — Prefer familiar, precise words

Replace ornate wording with common words when meaning survives. Keep established
technical terms naming necessary concepts. “Newborns” may simplify “neonatal
population,” but preserve eligibility or age distinctions if they matter.

### P18 — Be specific and concrete

Replace vague labels such as “important” with the property justifying them. Clarify
referents, comparisons, studies, and periods when needed. If the date or detail is
absent, identify the gap rather than fabricate specificity.

### P19 — Use jargon and acronyms purposefully

Keep needed technical terms and explain unfamiliar ones. Permit useful, defined
specialist acronyms, not just universally known ones. Define for audience, journal
rules, and independent units such as abstract and legends. Do not redefine in every
section automatically.

### P20 — Prefer accountable active voice

Use explicit actors when clearer. Keep passives for unknown/irrelevant actors,
object emphasis, or a pertinent convention. Do not infer an authorial actor from
an agentless passive. Identify who actually performed the action first.

### P21 — Use personal pronouns honestly

Use “we” or “I” to make real authorial actions visible. Impersonal wording is not
inherently objective. Match pronouns to authorship and agency; external laboratory
work must not become the authors' personal action.

### P22 — Let verbs carry actions

Prefer “we analyzed” to “we performed an analysis” when equivalent. Preserve
technical nouns and legitimate concepts. A noun followed by “of” is a candidate
for inspection, never proof of a nominalization requiring removal.

### P23 — Keep grammatical relationships visible

Keep subject and verb close enough to follow; reduce interruptions and heavy
subjects where burdensome. Place modifiers and pronouns near clear referents.
Counts can help triage, not enforce a 12-word limit.

### P24 — Be direct; retain essential negatives

Simplify double negatives and empty “there is/are” constructions when exact meaning
survives. Prefer positive forms when clearer, preserving necessary negative scope.
“Not statistically significant” must not become “no effect” or “equivalent.”

### P25 — Keep warranted uncertainty

Remove empty qualifiers, preserving “may,” “suggests,” “estimated,” and “approximately”
where warranted. Never strengthen certainty, erase measurement limitations, or turn
an estimate into an exact observation for stylistic economy.

### P26 — Vary sentences for comprehension

Combine repetitive related sentences and split overloaded ones. Vary structures
when helpful to rhythm and emphasis, without mechanical alternation. Similar lengths
alone are not defects. Keep complexity needed for qualifications and relations.

### P27 — Keep coordinated forms parallel

Use equivalent grammatical forms in pairs, lists, and comparisons: nouns, verbs,
gerunds, or complete clauses as appropriate. Describe comparable procedures and
groups consistently without altering data to create apparent symmetry.

### P28 — Use punctuation purposefully

Use colons for explanations/lists, semicolons for related independent clauses or
complex lists, parentheses for secondary information, and dashes sparingly for
emphasis or interruption. In English, colons generally follow complete clauses.
Follow the language's syntax; punctuation cannot rescue an overloaded sentence.

### P29 — Choose enough useful examples

Select representative, relevant examples sufficient to explain the idea. Three may
make a balanced list but are not a quota. Do not delete necessary examples or
invent new ones to meet a number.

### P30 — Follow language conventions

Check spelling, agreement, and references. In English, inspect affect/effect,
principal/principle, data agreement, restrictive/nonrestrictive clauses, and
comparisons contextually. Do not ban singular “they,” call “that” generically a
preposition, or universalize compared with/to preferences. Use verified journal style.

### P31 — Choose tense by function

Use past for completed work and observed findings; present for what a table shows
and knowledge presented as current. Distinguish what earlier researchers did from
what findings suggest. Do not force one tense across all rhetorical functions.

## Sections and displays: P32–P42

### P32 — Respect sections and genre

Use Introduction for question/context, Methods for procedures, Results for findings,
and Discussion for meaning when appropriate. Avoid misplaced and duplicate material.
Accommodate combined sections, protocols, editorials, and other valid formats.

### P33 — Move from knowledge to gap to objective

Focus Introduction on the question; synthesize relevant literature, identify the
gap, and state aim/question/hypothesis explicitly. Do not enforce a paragraph count.
Reserve extensive procedures and own-study interpretation for appropriate sections.

### P34 — Describe methods adequately

Report design, materials, participants, conditions, procedures, measures, and
analyses; include dose, temperature, and pH when relevant. Report applicable ethical
approval. Use headings, diagrams, and references without omitting adaptations.
Flag absent descriptions; never invent procedures/approvals or certify reproducibility.

### P35 — Summarize findings without reciting displays

Highlight principal comparisons, patterns, and informative numbers; leave details
accessible in displays. Include relevant negative, control, and inconclusive
findings. Use informative subheadings and keep interpretation separate when required.
Avoid selective reporting that favors a desired story.

### P36 — Match numerical precision to purpose

Choose numbers needed for the main message; keep detail accessible. Approximate
only when suitable and permitted, preserving required exactness. Check units,
denominators, rounding, and significant figures. Flag discrepancies rather than
invent values or run unrequested analyses.

### P37 — Discuss the answer

Start Discussion with the main answer and meaning. Develop relevant secondary
findings, literature context, and possible mechanisms. Distinguish observation,
interpretation, and hypothesis. Do not invent novelty, mechanisms, or references.

### P38 — State limits and proportionate implications

Discuss meaningful strengths/limitations and their consequences. Identify open
questions and supported implications. Conclude in proportion to the objective and
evidence, avoiding unsupported generalization, causation, speculation, or recommendations.

### P39 — Make the abstract faithful and independent

Update from the completed paper when available. Cover context, aim, essential methods,
main findings with decisive numbers, and supported conclusion. Follow the known
journal format and limit. If only an abstract is supplied, never claim consistency
with unseen sections or tables.

### P40 — Choose displays by purpose

Use tables for exact values/detail, graphs for patterns/relations, primary images
for visual evidence, and diagrams for processes/models. Avoid unnecessary duplication,
not every overlap. An overloaded figure may need splitting or redesign rather than
automatic conversion to a table.

### P41 — Make displays self-contained

Provide titles, legends, labels, units, groups, notes, and acronym definitions
sufficient for independent reading, consistent with the text. Do not repeat the
whole Discussion in legends or invent missing units and definitions.

### P42 — Protect visual legibility and integrity

Reduce grid clutter, arbitrary colors, confusing symbols, and misalignment. Inspect
labels, overlap, scales, and emphasis contextually; do not require a zero baseline
for every plot. Disclose text-only/caption-only review rather than imply image access.

## Other genres: P43–P44

### P43 — Use narrative to explain evidence

For essays and public communication, organize context, explanations, evidence, and
questions clearly. Use accurate vivid language and coherent metaphors where helpful.
Return to an opening theme when appropriate, without imposing storytelling on all
empirical manuscripts.

### P44 — Demonstrate personal qualities with evidence

Show identity, motivation, experience, goals, and specific fit through examples,
not a CV recital or trait list. Explain relevant setbacks honestly. Connect opening
and conclusion when useful. Never invent achievements, hardship, stories, or
institutional fit.
